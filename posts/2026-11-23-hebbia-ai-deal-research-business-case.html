Hebbia’s 30–40 Hours Saved: An AI Deal-Research Business Case

Hebbia’s document-research workflow shows why cited, reviewable outputs—not a clever chat response—are the unit of value in high-stakes AI adoption.

AI’s most valuable work is often hidden inside a document pile. For an investment bank, private-equity team, or law firm, a deal can mean thousands of pages of financial records, contracts, diligence materials, and prior work product. The hard part is not merely finding a keyword. It is comparing evidence across documents, producing a usable answer, and making every conclusion easy for a professional to check.

That is the problem Hebbia’s Matrix platform is aimed at. In a recently published OpenAI customer story, Hebbia reports that investment bankers save 30–40 hours per deal on activities such as marketing materials, meeting preparation, and counterparty responses. It reports 20–30 hours saved per deal for private-equity screening and due diligence, while law firms using its workflow have reduced credit-agreement review time by 75%. Those are company-reported outcomes, rather than independently audited benchmarks, but the workflow behind them is a strong model for business AI.

Make the Output Reviewable

In high-stakes work, a fluent answer is not the finish line. The reviewer needs to know which clause, table, or source supports it. Hebbia’s approach breaks a large question into analytical steps, processes full documents rather than only small retrieved excerpts, and returns cited outputs that can be inspected and corrected.

That changes the role of the human expert. Instead of spending the first pass gathering facts from a virtual data room, the banker or lawyer can spend it testing the analysis, following the citations, and deciding what matters. The machine accelerates coverage; the professional retains responsibility for the judgment.

For consequential AI work, the useful deliverable is not an answer. It is an answer with a visible path back to the evidence.

Design Around a Real Work Product

The strongest adoption detail here is that Matrix is not positioned as a general-purpose conversation layer. It is used to make things professionals already need: investment-committee memos, contract analyses, deal screening grids, and response materials. Each has a known audience, a deadline, a quality bar, and a human owner.

That is a better implementation target than a broad mandate to “use AI for research.” Choose one recurring work product, document the inputs that feed it, define the output format, and assign a reviewer. A system can then be evaluated on whether it finds the relevant information, represents it accurately, follows source citations, and reduces the effort needed to reach an acceptable draft.

Use Orchestration to Match the Work

Complex document work rarely has one step. It can require extracting covenants, reconciling dates, comparing entity names, calculating a ratio, and drafting a plain-language explanation. Hebbia describes a multi-agent setup that decomposes the job, routes tasks to different models, and synthesizes the result. The point is not that every business needs “agents.” It is that a production workflow should reflect the steps people actually take.

For a Havlek-style implementation, map the decision path before choosing the model. Separate retrieval, transformation, calculation, drafting, and approval. Keep structured data and authoritative source files available to the workflow rather than asking a model to reconstruct them from memory. Then preserve the intermediate evidence needed to troubleshoot an error.

Measure More Than Drafting Speed

The reported hours per deal make a clear capacity case, but leaders should avoid treating time saved as automatic value. Track the baseline effort, time to first reviewable draft, correction rate, missed issues, and the cycle time from intake to decision. In a deal context, the more strategic question is what the reclaimed time enables: more opportunities screened, deeper diligence, faster client responses, or fewer expensive external-review hours.

Hebbia also reports a 92% accuracy result on its own benchmark, compared with 68% for out-of-the-box retrieval-augmented generation. That comparison should be read as a platform claim, not a universal result. Its practical lesson is still valuable: build an evaluation set from representative documents and questions before scaling. A workflow should demonstrate quality on the edge cases the business actually cares about.

The Havlek Takeaway

Hebbia’s case shows where AI adoption can create serious leverage: not by removing the expert from a high-stakes decision, but by giving that expert a faster, traceable first pass across an impossible volume of information. Start with a repeatable work product. Design the system to return evidence alongside conclusions. Put a named reviewer in the loop, measure quality as well as throughput, and convert recovered time into a business outcome the team can own.
